Rendering and lighting

Empire Engine draws with Vulkan using physically based shading. A scene's look comes from its models' materials, its lights, its Environment (sky and ambient light) and its camera.

How surfaces are shaded

Every model is shaded with the Cook-Torrance model (GGX distribution, Smith geometry, Schlick Fresnel), the same family of shading that glTF and most modern engines use. From each material it reads:

  • Base color, metallic and roughness, each multiplied by its texture map
  • A normal map, which bends the surface normal for fine detail. Models don't need tangent data; tangents are worked out per pixel.
  • Occlusion, which darkens creases
  • Emission, which glows on its own and isn't affected by lights
  • IOR and specular (from the glTF extensions), which change how much non-metals reflect

Each surface is lit by the sun, up to 16 other lights, flat ambient light, and light from the sky (soft light from above, and reflections of the sky that blur as roughness rises). A Mesh's Tint multiplies the model's color.

See Models, textures and sound files for which material settings each file format carries.

In the editor, each light has a marker in the scene. A selected light also shows its reach: rings for a point light, the cone for a spot light, an arrow for a directional light.

The sun and the light limit

  • The sun is the scene's first Directional Light. It's drawn separately, and it is the only light that casts shadows.
  • On top of the sun, up to 16 point, spot and extra directional lights are drawn at once. When there are more, extra directional lights always count, and the point and spot lights nearest the camera win.
  • If a scene has no directional light at all, play mode and built games add a default sun so there is always light.

Shadows

The sun casts shadows into a shadow map centered a little ahead of the camera, so what's near you and what you're looking at are covered. Shadow edges are softened by blending nine shadow samples, fade out toward the edge of the covered area instead of ending in a line, and stay still as the camera moves.

Sky and ambient light

An Environment component sets what's drawn behind the scene and how much light reaches every surface regardless of the lights. Only the first one in the scene is used. Add one from the Hierarchy's right-click menu (Environment (sky and ambient)).

Sky modes

  • Color: one flat Sky Color.
  • Gradient (default): blends from Ground Color below the horizon, through Horizon Color, to Sky Color overhead.
  • Panorama: an equirectangular image (PNG, JPEG or Radiance .hdr) picked from the project. Until it loads the sky shows Sky Color. HDR panoramas are read as linear light.

Sky Intensity brightens or dims the sky.

Ambient and sky lighting

  • Ambient Color × Ambient Intensity (default white × 0.08) is flat light added to every surface.
  • Sky Lighting (default 0.5) is how much the sky itself lights the scene: soft light from above, the horizon and the ground on every surface, and reflections of the sky on shiny ones. 0 turns it off.

A scene without an Environment draws a dark blue backdrop with a little flat ambient light and no sky lighting.

Transparency

Materials can be cut out (glTF alpha mode MASK, for leaves and fences) or see-through (BLEND, for glass and water). See-through surfaces are drawn after all solid ones, blended over what's already drawn. They're hidden behind solid surfaces but don't hide each other, so several panes of glass in a row all show.

Cameras

Play mode and built games look through the scene's first Camera, in Hierarchy order. If there is none, play mode adds one. The camera looks along its object's −Z axis.

Anti AliasingWhat it does
NoneNo smoothing: the sharpest and fastest, with jagged edges.
MSAADraws several samples per pixel and averages them. Smooths the edges of shapes only, and needs the most GPU memory.
FXAAWorks on the finished picture: it finds edges from the brightness around each pixel and blurs along them. Cheap, and it also smooths edges inside textures, but it softens the picture a little.
SMAAAlso works on the finished picture, in three steps: find the edges, work out how to blend across each one, then blend. Cleaner and sharper than FXAA, and a little slower. This is SMAA 1x at the high preset, from the reference implementation by Jimenez et al. (MIT license).

FXAA and SMAA make the scene draw into a second picture the size of the window first, which is smoothed into the screen; SMAA uses two more for its in-between steps. Nothing is made until a camera asks for one of them. Only the scene is smoothed: the UI is drawn afterwards and stays sharp. A mesh your own Vulkan code draws during the scene is smoothed with the rest.

The main camera's anti-aliasing also applies to the editor's view. In the editor, cameras are drawn as a small body with lines showing what they see: yellow when selected, green for the camera play mode uses, gray otherwise.

The game view is 16:9. In a built game, F11 switches between a window and fullscreen.

Drawing with Vulkan yourself

For effects the engine doesn't provide, a Behaviour can record its own Vulkan commands every frame. Scripts are compiled with unsafe allowed and with the engine's Vulkan bindings referenced. Add using Engine.Brutal; (and using Brutal.VulkanApi; for the raw Vulkan types) and implement IVulkanRender:

using Engine.Brutal;
using Brutal.VulkanApi;

public class MyEffect : Behaviour, IVulkanRender
{
    // Inside the scene's rendering pass, after the engine's models, with the 16:9 viewport and scissor set.
    public void RenderVulkan(CommandBuffer cmd)
    {
        if (!Gpu.IsAvailable) return;
        // bind your own pipeline (made for Gpu.ColorFormat, Gpu.DepthFormat and Gpu.SampleCount) and draw
    }

    // Once when play stops or the game closes, after the GPU is idle. Destroy what you created.
    public void ReleaseVulkan() { }
}
